Title: Food Allergies (FDA) and Avoiding Food Allergy Reactions (FoodSafety.gov)
Source: U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA); FoodSafety.gov
URL: https://www.fda.gov/food/food-labeling-nutrition/food-allergies
URL: https://www.foodsafety.gov/blog/avoiding-food-allergy-reactions
Checked: November 2026

The FDA recognizes nine major food allergens: milk, eggs, fish, crustacean shellfish, tree nuts, peanuts, wheat, soybeans and sesame.

Sesame became the ninth major food allergen under the FASTER Act, signed in April 2021. The labeling requirement for sesame took effect on January 1, 2023.

Packaged foods must name the food source of each major allergen on the label at least once, either in parentheses after the ingredient (for example "whey (milk)" or "lecithin (soy)") or in a "Contains" statement next to the ingredient list. Labels must name the specific tree nut, the species of fish and the species of crustacean shellfish.

Advisory statements such as "may contain" or "made in a facility that also processes" an allergen are voluntary. They are not required by law, so a product without one is not guaranteed to be free of cross-contact.

Cross-contact happens when an allergen gets into a food it is not meant to be in, for example through shared equipment such as a deli slicer, shared production lines, surfaces that were not cleaned well, or airborne dust.

Allergen labeling rules do not apply to food wrapped or sliced to order for a customer at the counter, such as deli-sliced meat or cheese. These items may not have an allergen label, so the customer should ask staff about ingredients and shared equipment, and staff should check the product's original package or the manufacturer's information.

If someone has an allergic reaction, they should stop eating the food right away, use emergency medication such as epinephrine if they have it, and get medical help. A mild reaction does not mean the allergy is mild.
